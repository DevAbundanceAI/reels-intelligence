Contrarian POV

AI is not going to replace your business. A competitor whose team uses it better than yours will, a little more every month.

They will out-market you, out-sell you, and deliver faster with half the team. It will not happen all at once. It compounds every month until one day you cannot compete.

The reality for most teams right now is one person using ChatGPT like a toy. Nothing wired into the actual work. Nothing measured.

Access to AI is not the advantage anymore, everyone has that. The difference is whether it is pointed at the one thing actually capping the business, and wired into the tools the team already uses every day.

If your team is already ahead of that curve, ignore this post. If there is a sliver of doubt in your stomach right now, that doubt is the thing worth looking at.

Lesson/Correction

Most business owners who try AI never move the needle, and it's not because the tools don't work. It's where they point them.

At every stage of growth there is one constraint capping the business. The mistake almost everybody makes is installing AI where it is easy instead of where the constraint actually is. Somebody automates a newsletter while leads sit six hours before anyone follows up.

When my team opens a new client's ad account, most have numbers that are partly fiction, and most do not even have a dashboard for their own KPIs. So the AI ends up reading numbers that were never true in the first place.

The order that works is simple: build the coworker with full context first, use it to find the one constraint, then build systems aimed only at that. Once the real constraint is solved, the whole machine speeds up. Everything else was wasted time.

Build Log

My business partner and I built a production AI software product in under three months with no developers. Then I set it aside.

What it was: an AI coworker that does the work instead of advising on it. A task goes in from Slack, it plans the job, runs the work through the tools the team already uses, and posts the result back for approval. Two non-engineers and AI, nothing more.

What building it taught me: the AI brain itself is rented, the same engine everyone has access to, so all the real value sat in the wrapping. Memory, approvals, one client walled off from the next. The hardest part was honesty. We disabled one of our own features because it was not real yet, and that turned out to be the most persuasive thing we ever did.

Why I set it aside: the people using it kept asking for the same thing, and it was not another tool. It was someone to put AI inside their business in the right order. So that is where my time went.

The product taught me more than it earned, and that was the point.

Challenge Faced

I co-founded a telehealth brand. The hardest part was not demand, the ads, or the site. It was what we were allowed to say, and where.

Health is a regulated category. What a product page may claim, which ad copy clears review, how testimonials can be used, all of it is constrained. We built the review into the workflow instead of bolting it on after, so ad copy is compliance flagged before it runs and claims get reviewed before they publish.

Certification for paid ads has a queue. Clean applicants clear in weeks. Marketing copy that needs rework can take months. So the expensive mistake is applying with the site as is, hoping it clears.

If you are launching in a regulated space, this is the part that stops most teams cold, and I would rather you hear it from someone who has already been through it than learn it the hard way yourself.

Lesson/Correction

There's a stat I want to use and can't. Almost every ad account has broken attribution, and I can't source that, so I say most.

It feels true because of what we actually see when we open a new client's ad account. The lead event fires when the page loads instead of when someone books. No tracking tags on half the ads. Two pixels arguing with each other. But most is what I can defend, and a specific percentage is not.

A second number died the same way. An old per-call cost figure got replaced with a concrete example job and its real numbers instead of a repeated industry average. Less punchy. True.

In a market this full of AI claims, the checkable specific is the only thing that still reads as honest. If a stranger said prove it, could you, on a screen? If not, soften the claim or show the mechanism instead.

Framework

One question tells you whether you own a business or a job. If you disappeared for 14 days, no phone, no Slack, what happens to revenue?

You already know your honest answer, you felt it the moment you read the question. The calls do not get closed because you close them. The leads do not get followed up because the follow-up lives in your head. The team waits because every decision runs through you.

Here is the uncomfortable part. It is not your team's fault. You built it this way, because you were competent enough to be the engine, and competence past the point where it scales becomes the ceiling.

Every founder I have watched pass this test had the same thing running underneath them: booking, qualifying, follow-up, and reporting, all running whether they were in the room or not.

Fourteen days, no phone. Whatever your honest answer is, that is the constraint.

Lesson/Correction

We took the word AI out of the first line of everything we send to a prospect. Everyone's inbox already has an AI pitch in it.

The confession: our first drafts led with what we do. AI this, automation that. That is exactly what every other message in their inbox already says, so it reads as noise before the second sentence even lands.

What replaced it: one true, specific observation about their own business. Their booking page fires the lead event when the page loads, not when someone actually picks a time, so the cost per call in their ads manager looks better than it really is.

The rule that came with it: every finding gets checked against a screenshot or a timestamp before it goes out, because a wrong one is not just embarrassing, it is a trust problem. No finding, no touch. AI comes up later, once they have seen we actually looked.

Contrarian POV

Your sales team should be order-takers. I know how that sounds. Let me explain before you close the app.

Right now your closers are doing three jobs at once: educating the lead, convincing the lead, and closing the lead. That is why your best rep is exhausted by Thursday.

Picture the other version. The ad already called out exactly who it is for. The funnel already made the case. The qualification already filtered out everyone who cannot buy. So the person on the call already believes. They booked because the marketing did the selling. That is a pre-sold appointment.

At that point, what is left for sales is confirming fit, handling the real questions, and taking the order. Not because your closers got lazy, but because the system in front of them finally got competent.

If your closers are grinding, the problem is not in the sales call. It is everything that happened before it.

Lesson/Correction

I rebuilt my personal site and left off the two most impressive numbers from my old one. I couldn't trace either on a call.

The confession: an old site of mine carried a big round total and a big round count that sounded great. Every case study I could actually document added up to a smaller number, and a buyer doing diligence reads both, so the vague claim damages the credible one.

The second thing I found: one case study listed a total and a monthly average, and the monthly average multiplied out to a different total than the one sitting right next to it. Monthly averages invite that kind of division, and lose it. So now it is totals only, durations instead of dates, and every number traces to something I can actually discuss on a call.

The placeholder testimonial went too. If it is not real and attributable, the section gets cut.

The site got shorter, and it got a lot easier to defend. Easier to defend turned out to be what people actually trust.
